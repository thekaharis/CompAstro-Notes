# Simulator Dependence

## What It Is

**Simulator dependence** is the systematic failure of inference pipelines trained or calibrated on one reionization simulator to generalize when applied to data generated by a different simulator. It is a leading theoretical systematic for HERA-, LOFAR-, and SKA-era 21cm parameter inference.

Formally: let $\hat{\theta}^{(A \to A)}$ be parameters inferred by a model trained and tested on simulator A, and $\hat{\theta}^{(A \to B)}$ be parameters from a model trained on A but applied to data from B. Simulator dependence means $\hat{\theta}^{(A \to B)} \neq \hat{\theta}^{(A \to A)}$ even when A and B are tuned to the same $\bar{x}_\text{HII}(z)$.

This is not merely a statistical fluctuation or a minor calibration offset—it represents a **structural failure** of inference frameworks to recognize that different codes implement fundamentally different physics on intermediate scales (bubble radius ~ 1-20 Mpc), producing morphologies that ML models and MCMC chains exploit to fit the data, but which are simulator-specific rather than universal.

## Why It Happens: The Physical Decomposition

The root cause is that different simulators implement different approximations for the sub-bubble physics and bubble growth algorithm. To understand this quantitatively, we decompose the 21cm signal in the saturated spin-temperature limit:

$$
\delta T_b(\mathbf{x}, z) \propto x_\text{HI}(\mathbf{x}, z) \left[1 + \delta_m(\mathbf{x}, z) - \frac{\partial_\parallel v_\parallel}{aH}\right]
$$

This can be rewritten as:

$$
\delta T_b(\mathbf{x}, z) = x_\text{HI}(\mathbf{x}, z) \left[1 + \delta_m(\mathbf{x}, z)\right] + x_\text{HI}(\mathbf{x}, z) \left[- \frac{\partial_\parallel v_\parallel}{aH}\right]
$$

The **gravity sector** (the density and velocity terms) is governed by the same gravitational physics in all simulations: the matter power spectrum $P_{mm}(k)$, the growth factor $D(z)$, and the redshift-space distortion parameter $\beta(z)$ are determined by the cosmological model and are **shared across all codes**. These terms are well-constrained by other observations (galaxy surveys, weak lensing, CMB).

The **ionization sector** (the $x_\text{HI}(\mathbf{x}, z)$ spatial distribution) is **simulator-specific** because different codes make different choices about:

- **Source prescriptions**: how galaxies are identified, whether stellar mass is fixed or varies with halo mass, how stellar populations are sampled
- **Escape fraction treatment**: whether $f_\text{esc}$ is constant, mass-dependent, redshift-dependent, or is implicit in the ionizing efficiency $\zeta$
- **Recombination modelling**: whether to use a constant clumping factor $C$ or to compute clumping self-consistently; how partial ionization is handled
- **Bubble growth algorithm**: 
  - **Excursion-set codes** (21cmFAST, SCRIPT) identify ionized regions using a criterion on the integrated source mass above a spatial point; assumes spherical growth
  - **Conditional luminosity function** (SCRIPT variant) probabilistically assigns sources; better models source variability within halos
  - **Full radiative transfer** (THESAN, C²-Ray) explicitly propagates photons; accounts for shadowing, geometry-dependence, and anisotropic photon mean free path
- **Density field evolution**: 2LPT (semi-numerical codes, accurate only to $k \lesssim 0.5 h/\text{Mpc}$) vs. full N-body (semi-analytic codes, accurate to $k \lesssim 0.1 h/\text{Mpc}$ typically) vs. hydrodynamics with radiative transfer (THESAN, most accurate but only ~100 Mpc volumes)

The **key insight**: these differences produce different ionization morphologies $x_\text{HII}(\mathbf{x}, z)$ even when the global history $\bar{x}_\text{HII}(z)$ is matched. An ML model trained on code A learns the morphological patterns of A and mistakes them for universal physics. When the model encounters morphologies from code B, which are structured differently even at matched reionization history, it produces biased inferences on the astrophysical parameters ($\zeta$, $T_\text{vir}$, $R_\text{mfp}$).

## The Hierarchy of Simulator Differences

Differences between codes are not all equal. There is a hierarchy of physical approximation gaps:

1. **Full radiative transfer (THESAN, C²-Ray) vs. semi-numerical (21cmFAST, SCRIPT):** This is the **largest gap**. Full RT solves the transport equation for photon energy and flux; semi-numerical uses excursion-set or luminosity-function heuristics. The morphologies can differ in bubble size distributions and small-scale clustering by factors of 2-3 or more. At large scales ($k < 0.1 h/\text{Mpc}$), EFT coefficients converge, but intermediate scales (~1-20 Mpc) are affected.

2. **Within semi-numerical family** (21cmFAST vs. SCRIPT vs. AMBER): Even among excursion-set codes, differences in:
   - How $R_\text{eff}$ is computed (mean free path approximation, spherical vs. actual topology)
   - Source variability treatment (SCRIPT's conditional luminosity function vs. 21cmFAST's fixed source masses per halo mass)
   - Partial ionization handling
   
   produce morphological differences of order **20-50%** in bubble size distributions and ionization field gradients. These are smaller than full RT vs. semi-numerical, but still significant enough to affect EFT coefficients ($b_{\nabla^2}^x$ is especially sensitive).

3. **Same code, different model choices** (Berklas & Pober 2025): Even holding the code fixed, varying internal model parameters (clumping factor parameterization, recombination treatment, source mass thresholds) biases inference posteriors by order **1-2 σ** for standard parameter surveys.

## Empirical Evidence: Three Quantitative Examples

Three papers establish simulator dependence at different levels with concrete numbers:

### 1. Zhou & La Plante 2022: CNN Cross-Code Failure
- **Architecture:** Convolutional neural network trained on 21cmFAST lightcone images
- **Training set:** 21cmFAST simulations with varied $\zeta$, $T_\text{vir}$, $R_\text{mfp}$
- **Test set:** Data from `zreion` code (semi-numerical, assignment-based)
- **Finding:** CNN trained on 21cmFAST → trained model applied to zreion data produces **systematic bias of ~30%** in inferred $\zeta$ (the ionizing efficiency parameter). The model predicts systematically high $\zeta$ for zreion data because it has learned morphological signatures of 21cmFAST that zreion does not replicate.
- **Interpretation:** The CNN learned to associate certain spatial patterns (bubble size distribution, clustering structure) with values of $\zeta$. When applied to zreion, which has different bubble morphologies at the same global $\bar{x}_\text{HII}(z)$, the inferred parameter is biased.

### 2. Berklas & Pober 2025: Within-Code Model Dependence
- **Architecture:** 21CMMC MCMC pipeline (standard Bayesian inference on 21cmFAST)
- **Setup:** Vary internal 21cmFAST model choices (clumping factor as constant vs. depth-dependent; recombination prescription; source mass threshold)
- **Finding:** Changing model choices within the same code shifts inferred posteriors by **1-2 σ** (one to two standard deviations). The 68% credible intervals for $\zeta$ and $T_\text{vir}$ shift by amounts comparable to or larger than statistical uncertainties.
- **Interpretation:** The global reionization history $\bar{x}_\text{HII}(z)$ is insensitive to these model choices (all variants tuned to match the same history), but the 21cm power spectrum and higher moments are sensitive. MCMC exploits these power spectrum differences to constrain parameters, but different model choices produce different "effective" parameter-to-power-spectrum mappings, biasing the posterior.

### 3. Sooknunan et al. 2024: ML Reproducibility Survey
- **Setup:** Systematic test of 10+ different ML architectures (CNNs, ViTs, normalizing flows, graph neural networks) on 21cm inference
- **Cross-simulator test:** Train on 21cmFAST, test on SCRIPT
- **Finding:** All architectures exhibit cross-simulator degradation. Mean square error rises by **factor of 2-5** when trained and tested on different codes. Some specific numbers:
  - In-code test (21cmFAST → 21cmFAST): R² = 0.90-0.95 for parameter estimation
  - Cross-code test (21cmFAST → SCRIPT): R² drops to 0.35-0.55
  - F1 scores for reionization phase classification drop from 0.85 (in-code) to 0.50-0.65 (cross-code)
- **Interpretation:** The problem is **pervasive**: it affects CNNs, RNNs, attention mechanisms, graph networks—any architecture that learns from 21cm data trained on one code will fail on another. This indicates the failure is not a quirk of one architecture, but reflects fundamental structural differences in the data.

## Why Standard Validation Masks the Problem

A critical but often-overlooked failure mode of current ML pipelines is that **train/test splits within the same simulator give misleadingly optimistic performance metrics**.

When you train an ML model on 21cmFAST data and evaluate on a separate test set of 21cmFAST data (same code, different random seeds or parameter values), you measure:

$$
R^2_{\text{in-code}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i^{\text{true}} - y_i^{\text{pred}})^2}{\sum_i (y_i^{\text{true}} - \bar{y})^2}
$$

This quantity can be very high (R² > 0.95 is common) because the model learns the morphological features of 21cmFAST perfectly. But this tells you nothing about whether the model has learned **universal physics** or **code-specific features**.

When you apply the same model to SCRIPT data (different code, but same global history and cosmology), you measure:

$$
R^2_{\text{cross-code}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i^{\text{SCRIPT}} (y_i^{\text{true}} - y_i^{\text{pred}})^2}{\sum_i^{\text{SCRIPT}} (y_i^{\text{true}} - \bar{y})^2}
$$

This can drop to R² ~ 0.3-0.6 because SCRIPT's morphologies are different enough to fool the 21cmFAST-trained model.

**The danger:** if your only validation metric is in-code R², you will think your model is excellent and ready for real SKA data. If you apply it to real data (which will almost certainly have morphological signatures not present in any training simulator), you will produce biased posteriors. Cross-simulator validation is essential but not routinely done in the field.

## The Shared vs. Simulator-Specific Decomposition

The decomposition of the 21cm signal into a **shared gravity sector** and a **simulator-specific ionization sector** is the key to understanding where simulator dependence arises:

**Shared part (all codes agree):**
- The matter density field $\delta_m(\mathbf{x}, z)$ is determined by gravity + the initial conditions
- The velocity field and its divergence $\partial_\parallel v_\parallel$ follow from the continuity equation
- These terms contribute ~50% of the 21cm power at large scales (k < 0.1 h/Mpc) in most redshift ranges

**Simulator-specific part (codes disagree):**
- The ionization pattern $x_\text{HII}(\mathbf{x}, z)$ is determined by source placement, photon budget, recombinations, and bubble overlap
- At large scales, the ionization pattern can be expanded in the EFT basis (see [[Effective Field Theory]])
- But the coefficients of this expansion vary between codes because they encode the sub-bubble physics

The implication: if you want a **simulator-independent** inference target, you must parameterize the ionization sector in a way that is independent of the specific bubble-growth algorithm. The EFT coefficients $\{b_1^x, b_2^x, b_{\nabla^2}^x, P_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\}$ are defined by the physical operators (biases, stochasticity), not by any simulator.

## Current Mitigation Strategies and Their Limitations

### Multi-Simulator Training (Empirical)

[[Solt et al 2026 (Multi-Simulator Training)]]: Train on pooled data from multiple simulators simultaneously. Results show that cross-simulator generalization improves when the training set contains multiple codes.

**Strengths:**
- Empirically demonstrated to improve out-of-distribution performance
- Requires no new theory, just more training data

**Limitations:**
- No physical basis for what is being universalized; the learned representations may be arbitrary averaging effects
- Typically only works well when training set contains the target simulator (or a very similar one)
- Destroys spatial information by compressing to scalar summaries like $\Delta z$ (the 21cm equivalent of the $21\,\text{cm}$ signal integrated along the line of sight); loses information about power spectrum shape
- Does not identify *why* generalization improves or what physical aspects are being captured
- If a new code emerges with different morphological signatures, retraining is required

### EFT Coefficients (Principled — Thesis Approach)

The thesis proposes using EFT coefficients $\{b_1^x, b_2^x, b_{\nabla^2}^x, P_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\}$ as inference targets instead of native simulator parameters. These have fundamental advantages:

**Advantages:**
- **Physical definition:** The operators are defined by symmetries (isotropy, Galilean invariance), not by any simulator. Different codes are UV completions of the same EFT—they agree on the operator basis but disagree on coefficients.
- **Common language across codes:** All codes produce ionization fields that must obey the same large-scale symmetries, so all must have the same operator basis. The coefficients encode what's different.
- **Interpretability:** Each coefficient has a direct physical meaning:
  - $b_1^x$ encodes source bias and the mean ionized fraction
  - $b_2^x$ encodes patchiness and radiation-opacity coupling
  - $b_{\nabla^2}^x \propto R_\text{eff}^2$ encodes the effective bubble radius
  - $P_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}$ encodes bubble discreteness and stochasticity
- **Richer than scalar summaries:** Full coefficient trajectories $\{b_1^x(z), \ldots\}$ over redshift contain more information than $\Delta z$ alone.
- **Potential for true generalization:** If EFT coefficients are more stable across codes than native parameters, then inference targeting coefficients should generalize better because you are explicitly modeling the representation that is universal.

**Challenges:**
- Extracting EFT coefficients requires fitting the bias expansion in Fourier space, which introduces systematic errors if the EFT regime is exceeded
- Coefficients are redshift-dependent; a full inference pipeline would need to model coefficient evolution
- Limited existing work on whether EFT coefficients really are more stable across codes (this is exactly what P1 measures)

### Self-Supervised Representations (Data-Driven)

[[Ore et al 2025 (SKATR)]]: Self-supervised ViT learns simulator-agnostic representations empirically. The method:
- Trains a Vision Transformer on unlabeled 21cm lightcone images using contrastive learning (e.g., simclr)
- Obtains learned feature representations that are invariant to simulator choice
- Uses these features as input to a second-stage SBI model

**Strengths:**
- Empirically demonstrated to achieve cross-simulator generalization
- No theoretical assumptions needed; learns from data

**Limitations:**
- Lacks interpretability; features are not connected to physics concepts
- Requires large unlabeled training set to learn good representations
- Complementary to EFT approach but not competitive; combines well with EFT

## The Correct Representation: EFT as a Universality Hypothesis

The core insight is that **EFT coefficients provide a principled answer to the question: what is the correct simulator-independent language for reionization?**

Different simulators are different **UV completions** of the same large-scale EFT. This is analogous to how different particle physics models (technicolor, supersymmetry, extra dimensions) at the TeV scale all produce the same effective low-energy Standard Model at GeV scales if tuned correctly.

More precisely:
- **UV completion = simulator algorithm** (21cmFAST's excursion-set, SCRIPT's conditional luminosity function, THESAN's radiative transfer)
- **EFT = large-scale ionization field** (what observable experiments see)
- **Operator basis** = $\{1, \delta_m, \delta_m^2, \nabla^2\delta_m, \varepsilon\}$ (same for all codes)
- **Coefficients** = $\{b_1^x(z), b_2^x(z), b_{\nabla^2}^x(z), P_{\varepsilon\varepsilon}(k,z)\}$ (differ between codes because they encode simulator-specific sub-bubble physics)

> [!key-insight]
> Simulator dependence is not a bug to be patched around — it reflects real physical differences in how codes implement sub-bubble physics. The EFT parameterizes these differences explicitly and provides a common language across codes. The crucial question P1 answers is: how stable are EFT coefficients across simulators? Do they provide genuine universality, or are coefficient values also simulator-dependent?

## Analogy to Other Simulation-Based Inference Problems in Physics

Simulator dependence is not unique to 21cm cosmology. It arises in any field where ML inference is applied to simulated data:

### Jet simulation dependence (LHC physics)
- **Problem:** Monte Carlo event generators (Pythia, Herwig, Sherpa) use different hadronization and parton-shower models, producing different jet morphologies at the same underlying hard process (quark/gluon).
- **Impact:** ML classifiers trained on Pythia jets show systematic biases when applied to Herwig data; affects search for new physics.
- **Mitigation:** Use generator-agnostic feature spaces (e.g., substructure moments independent of parton shower details). Similar in spirit to our EFT approach.

### Gravitational wave waveform dependence (gravitational wave inference)
- **Problem:** Different numerical relativity codes and inspiral approximation schemes (Effective One Body, post-Newtonian, etc.) produce waveforms that agree to post-Newtonian order but differ in the merger regime.
- **Impact:** Inference on GW signals can be biased if trained on one waveform model but applied to data from another.
- **Mitigation:** Use parameterized waveform families that are marginalized over code/approximation choice.

### Hydrodynamics simulation dependence (galaxy formation)
- **Problem:** Different galaxy formation codes (ILLUSTRIS, EAGLE, FIRE) use different subgrid physics recipes (star formation, feedback), producing galaxies that match observations but have different internal structure.
- **Impact:** ML trained on one code's galaxies does not transfer to another.
- **Solution:** Physical parameterization of galaxy properties (e.g., galaxy bias for large-scale structure) that is code-independent.

The EFT approach to 21cm simulator dependence fits naturally into this broader pattern: when simulators can differ, learn universal physical parameterizations instead of simulator-specific features.

## Connection to Thesis

This concept is the central problem the thesis addresses:

- **P1: Characterization** measures and validates the EFT description of $x_\text{HII}$ across codes — establishing whether the common language exists and quantifying how much simulator variation it captures. Key results will be:
  - Coefficient trajectories $\{b_1^x(z), b_2^x(z), b_{\nabla^2}^x(z)\}$ extracted from 21cmFAST and SCRIPT with matched ICs
  - How much do these trajectories differ between codes at matched $\bar{x}_\text{HII}(z)$?
  - At what $k$-scales does the EFT break down for each code?
  
- **P2: Inference** tests whether inferring EFT coefficients instead of native parameters improves cross-simulator generalization in practice:
  - Train SBI model (e.g., swyft) targeting EFT coefficients on 21cmFAST
  - Test on SCRIPT data
  - Compare cross-simulator R² to baseline (inference on $\zeta, T_\text{vir}, R_\text{mfp}$)
  - Hypothesis: EFT-targeting inference should achieve R² > 0.7 cross-simulator vs. R² < 0.5 for native parameters

## Why the Problem Is Acute: Implications for Real Data

The problem is acute because of a simple fact: **the real Universe is not simulated by any single code.** Real SKA and HERA data will reflect the actual physical processes that reionize the Universe, which may differ from any simulator's assumptions in subtle ways.

**Three failure scenarios:**

1. **Unknown sub-bubble physics:** Some physics not included in any code (e.g., very detailed multiphase IGM structure, molecular hydrogen feedback, primordial black hole radiation) could affect the ionization morphology in ways the training data doesn't capture.

2. **Model mismatch on intermediate scales:** The scales where simulators differ most (1-20 Mpc) are exactly where SKA will have excellent sensitivity. If your inference model learned code-specific features on these scales, it will be wrong for real data.

3. **Parameter-dependent morphology evolution:** The morphology of reionization changes with redshift and parameter values in code-specific ways. Multi-simulator training can reduce this bias, but only if the training set covers the parameter space relevant to the real Universe.

**Why EFT helps:** By parameterizing the ionization field in terms of physical operators and their coefficients, you make the following claim: the large-scale ionization properties that SKA/HERA will measure are governed by a small number of bias coefficients, regardless of the simulator used to calibrate them. If this is true, then inferring coefficients should be robust to sub-bubble physics variations not captured in simulations.

## Scale of the Problem: Quantitative Summary

- **In-code cross-validation R²:** 0.90-0.95 (misleadingly optimistic)
- **Cross-code R² (21cmFAST ↔ SCRIPT):** 0.35-0.60 (substantial degradation)
- **Parameter bias (21cmFAST → zreion):** Δζ ~ 30% systematic bias (Zhou & La Plante 2022)
- **Within-code model variation:** 1-2 σ posterior shift (Berklas & Pober 2025)
- **Morphological difference hierarchy:**
  - Full RT vs. semi-numerical: ~3× bubble size difference
  - Within semi-numerical (21cmFAST vs. SCRIPT): ~20-50% RMS difference in $b_{\nabla^2}^x$
  - Same code, different recipes: ~1-2 σ parameter shift

These numbers show that simulator dependence is not a 5% effect—it is a dominant systematic that can shift inferred parameters by 20-50% or more, comparable to statistical uncertainties in current and near-future experiments. Any inference pipeline that doesn't explicitly account for it will produce biased posteriors on real data.
